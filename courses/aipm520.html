AIPM 520 — AI Product Craft: Feasibility, Evals & Delivery

The signature judgments of the AI PM: when AI is the right tool, how the system should be shaped, how quality is operated in production, what it costs, and what could go wrong. Three of the program's four cases live here.

Course objectives
Judge feasibility — AI vs. rules vs. human vs. nothing — with a repeatable drill.
Sketch a composable AI architecture: retrieval, guardrails, human-in-the-loop.
Design a production eval system — golden sets, regression gates, drift.
Model cost and latency, and select models with explicit trade-offs.
Produce a risk register and a go-to-market one-pager.
Modules
M1Feasibility & system design · Case B: Intercom Fin

Feasibility is the AI PM's signature judgment: should this use AI at all? The test is comparative — AI vs. a rules-based system vs. a human vs. nothing — scored on five dimensions: input/output clarity, error tolerance (what happens when it's wrong?), data availability, cost per interaction, and latency. AI wins when the task is high-volume, language-shaped, and error-tolerant; it loses when answers must be deterministic, when the cost of being wrong is catastrophic, or when the problem is actually about process, not intelligence. An "AI-powered toaster" is not a product; it's a gimmick with a transformer inside.

When AI is right, the architecture should be boring: model + retrieval + tools + guardrails + a human in the loop where stakes demand it. The Fin case (packet B) is the masterclass: retrieval-only answers (no improvisation from training knowledge), source links (verifiable answers), policy checks as separate model calls rather than one giant prompt, an explicit human handoff for high-stakes actions, and a phased rollout (customers like Rocket Money started at 10% of conversations with tightly scoped workflows). The lesson from Anthropic's Building Effective Agents: the most successful systems are simple, composable patterns, not complex frameworks — because simple is how you keep a probabilistic system debuggable.

M2Architecture: retrieval, guardrails, tool use

The architecture canvas for an LLM product has four load-bearing parts. Retrieval grounds answers in your documents — never the model's memory — and defines what the system may know (AI 510 M4). Guardrails constrain behavior: scope (what topics are allowed), format (structured output), refusal behavior (what it must decline), and — the Fin lesson — policy checks as separate model calls ("verify this answer against the policy before sending") rather than one giant prompt, because separate calls are separately measurable and debuggable. Tools let the model act — search, compute, APIs — through function calling, with least-privilege access (a support agent doesn't hold the refund API until a human approves). Human-in-the-loop sits wherever an irreversible or high-stakes action happens.

The failure modes you must be able to name, because you will see them in production: retrieval misses (the right chunk didn't surface), context-position effects (Lost in the Middle), tool misuse (wrong tool, hallucinated arguments, loops), guardrail gaps (an out-of-scope question slips through), and silent degradation over time (M4 of this course). The drawing — boxes and arrows, with the human handoff marked — is the product spec. If you cannot draw it, you do not understand the system, and neither does anyone else.

M3Data: the fuel

Every AI product runs on data, and most product failures trace back to a data assumption made early and never revisited. Three distinctions organize the subject. First, quality vs. quantity: 1,000 clean, labeled examples beat 100,000 messy ones — the model learns the pattern, not the noise, and noise costs more than absence. Second, retrieval data vs. fine-tuning data: retrieval data is the corpus your RAG system looks up — you maintain it like documentation, and it is the default lever; fine-tuning data changes the model itself — expensive, riskier, and it carries the burden of proof. The default architecture is retrieval; fine-tuning is the exception you must argue for.

Third, the data flywheel: product usage generates data — queries, corrections, ratings, eval cases — that improves the product and its evaluation, which attracts more usage. The flywheel is the compounding strategic asset of an AI product; it is also why data governance is strategy, not compliance (full treatment in DATA 530). Privacy is the constraint that shapes all of it: know what PII is, know that user data is not yours to train on without consent, and know the vocabulary of GDPR and the EU AI Act even if you're not in Europe — they govern what global products may do.

M4Evals in production

AI 510 M6 taught you to measure; this module teaches you to operate. A shipped AI product degrades — the world changes, users find new edge cases, the distribution of inputs drifts — so quality is an ongoing operating problem, not a launch-day checkbox. The production system has five parts:

(1) A golden set — fixed cases that never change, so you can compare across model versions and catch regressions. (2) A scoring method — human review for high-stakes slices, LLM-as-judge for volume, with the discipline to spot-check the judge (judges have biases too). (3) Regression gates — "we do not ship a model version that scores below X" — the mechanism that turns evals into governance. (4) Drift monitoring — live signals (thumbs-down rate, "I don't know" rate, cost per session, rolling eval scores) that trigger a re-eval. (5) The feedback loop — bad answers from production become new eval cases, so the eval set learns with the product.

The speech to internalize and repeat to executives: "it worked in the demo" is not "it works in production." The demo is a single sample; production is a distribution. The person who owns the eval system owns the quality conversation — which is why this document is one of the five that carries the most weight in interviews.

M5Cost, latency & model selection · Case C: Duolingo Max

AI breaks the software assumption that marginal cost is ~zero: every interaction burns tokens, and your bill scales with success. The toolkit: cost per interaction = (input tokens × input price + output tokens × output price) + retrieval/storage; × expected usage = monthly bill. Latency is a product constraint — a "personal assistant" that takes ten seconds to respond is not a personal assistant — so set the latency budget before choosing the model. Model tiers are the trade-off engine: frontier models (better, slower, dearer) vs. small/cheap models (fast, cheap, good-enough on most cases) — and the professional move is to find where the cheap model is good enough, using your eval set, not the leaderboard. Caching, shorter contexts, and cheaper models for routine calls are the levers; fine-tuning is the expensive last resort.

The Duolingo case (packet C) is the cautionary tale that defines this module: AI features worked — Roleplay and Explain My Answer on GPT-4, with Birdbrain (classical ML) carrying the core experience — and yet gross margin compressed from 73.0% to 71.1% because compute scaled with success, while analysts cut the price target anyway. The lesson: budget for your own success before you launch — model tiering, caching, and packaging that aligns revenue with compute are not optimizations; they are the product strategy.

M6Risk, safety & go-to-market · Case D: Klarna

The risk taxonomy for AI products: hallucination (wrong confident output), bias (skewed training data producing skewed outcomes), privacy (PII leakage in prompts, training, and logs), security (prompt injection — attacker text hijacking the model's instructions), and compliance (GDPR, EU AI Act, sector rules). The instrument is the risk register: for each risk — likelihood, impact, mitigation, detection, owner. Detection is what turns "this is risky" into governance: "we mitigate hallucinations with retrieval" is a plan; "we sample 2% of sessions weekly against the golden set" is a system. (RSK 550 will go deep; this module gives you the working version.)

Go-to-market completes the picture: positioning — "the fastest way to resolve a refund" beats "AI-powered refund agent" — and pricing, where usage-based cost argues for usage-based price, hybrid tiers, or outcome pricing (Fin's "pay per resolution"). The Klarna case (packet D) teaches the epistemological layer: every headline number — 2.3M conversations, "the work of 700 agents," $40M — is vendor-published, and your job as a PM is the adversarial, constructive read: which claims would you trust, which would you discount, and what would you measure yourself. The AI PM's relationship to numbers is adversarial-by-default and constructive-by-design.
