DATA 530 — Data, Metrics & Experimentation for AI Products

The measurement spine, done rigorously: designing measurement systems that cannot be gamed, instrumenting them, running experiments that survive AI's quality problem, and governing the data itself.

Course objectives
Design a measurement system for any AI feature — north star, guardrails, taxonomies.
Specify instrumentation: events, properties, funnels, cohorts.
Design experiments where the treatment changes quality, not just clicks.
Govern data: PII, GDPR/EU AI Act vocabulary, quality operations, the flywheel.
Modules
M1Measurement systems design

A single metric is a hypothesis; a measurement system is a structure. It has three layers: a north star that captures delivered value, guardrails that must not silently degrade, and a taxonomy that connects them — a map of which metrics explain changes in which other metrics, so that when the north star moves you know why. The discipline that makes the whole thing trustworthy: every metric has a definition, an owner, and a stated failure mode. If you cannot say how a metric would mislead you, you have not defined it — "accuracy" misleads on skewed data, "resolution rate" misleads when users give up, "engagement" misleads when it's driven by novelty.

For AI features the taxonomy gains a dedicated class: model-quality metrics — eval score, hallucination rate, cost per session, latency percentiles — that live alongside product metrics and are treated as first-class, not as engineering details. The artifact is the metrics tree: north star at the top, drivers below, guardrails at the side. It is the document that makes success legible to executives, engineers, and you — and it is the thing you revisit when someone proposes celebrating a metric you know is lying.

a metric without a failure mode isn't definedmodel-quality metrics are first-class
Problem set
  1. For your capstone: write every metric in the tree with (a) exact definition, (b) owner, (c) its failure mode — how it could mislead.
  2. Add the model-quality metric class: eval score, hallucination rate, cost per session, latency p95.
  3. Draw the tree.

  • What does a metric with no failure mode cost a company?

M2Instrumentation & analytics foundations

Instrumentation is where measurement becomes real. The primitives: events (searched, asked, answered, rated, returned), properties on each event (model version, latency, tokens used, route taken), funnels (the ordered sequence showing where value leaks), and cohorts (does the October cohort retain like September's? — the question that funnels can't answer). The rule — "if it wasn't logged, it didn't happen" — makes instrumentation a product decision made before launch, not a post-hoc request to engineers.

The AI-specific extension: log the model version and eval context with every event. When you change the model, the logs must let you attribute changes in behavior to changes in the model — otherwise your product metrics and your model metrics are two stories that can't be reconciled, and every launch is a confounded experiment. Your deliverable this module is an event spec an engineer can implement verbatim: event names, properties, triggers, and the funnel definitions — build-ready, with no ambiguity about what "rated" means or when it fires.

log the model version with every eventfunnels + cohorts = the picturebuild-ready event spec
Problem set
  1. Write the full event spec for your capstone: every event, its properties (including model version), its trigger.
  2. Define the funnel as an ordered sequence with drop-off points you'd watch.
  3. Define one cohort question and the query shape that answers it.
Seminar

M3Experimentation for AI

A/B testing is the cleanest instrument in the toolkit — and the easiest to fool yourself with. The foundations: randomization (treatment and control differ only by the change), pre-registered hypotheses (write down the expected effect and the guardrails before you look at results), sample size and power (you need enough users to see the effect you care about — the smaller the effect, the longer the test), and a minimum duration that covers your usage cycles (a weekly-usage product needs more than a weekend test).

The AI-specific trap: an AI treatment changes quality, not just clicks. A chatbot that deflects more tickets but angers customers, a recommender that lifts clicks but narrows the world — the primary metrics look great while the guardrails bleed. So every AI experiment treats guardrail metrics (hallucination rate, cost per session, support contacts, retention) as primary outcomes, and pre-registers them. Watch also the novelty effect — new AI features get engagement simply for being new, and the effect decays — and selection effects in who sees the treatment. If the experiment cannot detect harm, it cannot license the feature.

pre-register the hypothesisguardrails are primary in AI testsnovelty effects lie
Problem set

  3. State the novelty-effect risk and how you'd measure decay.

M4Data governance & the flywheel

The data layer is an asset and a liability at once. The governance basics every PM needs: know what PII is and where it lives in your product; know the GDPR and EU AI Act vocabulary — consent, purpose limitation, transparency, risk classification — even if you're not in Europe, because they govern what global products may do; and internalize the rule that user data is not yours to train on without an explicit legal basis. Operationally, data quality is an ongoing practice with owners and monitoring like any production system: schema drift, silent gaps, label rot — each needs detection, or your eval set quietly decays.

The upside is the data flywheel: usage → feedback (ratings, corrections, eval cases) → better eval sets and better products → more usage. The flywheel is the moat that makes an AI product defensible — model capability is rented, but your feedback loop compounds. Governance is what keeps the flywheel legal and trusted; the two are not in tension, they are the same system. The deliverable this module — a governance checklist with a PII map and a flywheel plan — is the document that lets you say "we can scale this" without wincing.
