PM 501 — Product Management Fundamentals

The craft itself — discovery, specification, prioritization, metrics, and delivery — taught with AI products as the working examples. This is the half of the job that has nothing to do with models and everything to do with judgment.

Course objectives
Run non-leading discovery interviews and write JTBD statements.
Write PRDs with testable acceptance criteria engineers can build from.
Prioritize with RICE and build theme-based roadmaps with a "won't do" list.
Design a metrics tree — north star, guardrails, funnel, instrumentation.
Operate the delivery rituals: sprints, standups, retros, definition of done.
Modules
M1The PM's instrument & the double diamond

A product manager owns the answer to three questions: what problem are we solving, for whom, and how will we know it worked? Everything else — PRDs, roadmaps, standups — is instrumentation around those three. Note what the job is not: it is not writing features, not being the boss of engineers, not the loudest opinion. It is the person who makes the problem, the user, and the evidence legible to everyone, and who converts ambiguity into decisions.

The double diamond names the shape of good product work: discover the right problem (divergent — explore broadly) → define it (convergent — commit to a problem statement) → develop the right solution (divergent — generate options) → deliver it (convergent — ship and measure). Most failures happen in the first diamond, where speed feels like progress; the PM's discipline is refusing to converge on a solution before the problem is evidenced. The AI PM extends the frame by one actor: the model, which has capabilities, failure modes, and a per-token price — a fourth party in every conversation. This module also does the asset map: your strategist/operator experience (stakeholders, scoping, delivery, risk) translates directly into PM competencies. You have run most of this job; the course gives it vocabulary and instrument.

M2Discovery & user research

Discovery is an evidence-gathering discipline: you are looking for demonstrated behavior, not stated desire — what people actually did, how they solve the problem today, what it costs them in time, money, or pain. The core move from The Mom Test: talk about their past, never pitch your idea, never ask "would you use this?" — because people lie to be polite, and polite lies are the most expensive data in product development. Ask instead about the last time the problem happened, what they did, what they tried, what they paid.

The output is jobs-to-be-done — "when ___, I want ___, so I can ___" — and a set of direct quotes you can show. Three to five well-run interviews beat twenty shallow ones; the goal is to find the pattern and, more importantly, the surprises — the moments where reality contradicts your assumption. Those surprises are the gold: they are where the product opportunity actually lives. A problem statement without interview evidence is a hypothesis wearing a costume; your PRD in M3 will cite this evidence, which is what separates product management from opinion.

behavior, not vibesJTBDthe "surprises" section is the gold

  3. Capture: 5+ direct quotes, 3 JTBD statements, and a "surprises" section (what contradicted your assumption).

M3Spec writing: PRDs, stories, acceptance criteria

The PRD is a decision document, not a wish list: it records the problem (with evidence), the users, the success metrics, the scope, and the requirements in a form engineers can build from. The unit of requirements is the user story — "as a ___, I want ___, so ___" — with acceptance criteria written as given/when/then ("Given a question outside scope, when asked, the bot responds with the out-of-scope message"). Testable acceptance criteria are the difference between a spec and a vibe: an engineer can verify them, a tester can automate them, and a PM can defend them.

Scope is the PRD's spine. An explicit out-of-scope section is a promise to stakeholders about what you are not doing — harder and more valuable than listing what you are, because it is where scope creep lives. The quality test for the whole document: could an engineer build this without asking me ten questions? If not, tighten. For AI features, two extra sections matter: the model-quality requirements (what error rate is acceptable, what the refusal behavior is) and the evaluation plan — how the feature will be judged before and after launch (you'll deepen both in AIPM 520).

M4Prioritization & roadmaps

Saying yes to everything is free and worthless. Prioritization is where PMs earn their keep, because every yes is a no to something else, and the something else is invisible unless you write it down. RICE makes the trade explicit: Reach × Impact × Confidence ÷ Effort — a single number that forces you to write down assumptions. Confidence is the confession of uncertainty, which is its point: a 40%-confidence score on a big-impact item is a research assignment, not a decision. MoSCoW shapes a release (must / should / could / won't) once you know what's in the quarter.

The modern roadmap is organized by themes with outcomes — "improve activation for new users" — not by dated features, because themes survive contact with reality and dates don't. The most valuable artifact on any roadmap is the written "won't do" list with reasons: it is the difference between a strategy and an aspiration. For AI features, add the hidden costs to your RICE: model quality risk, eval effort, and per-interaction cost (AIPM 520 and STRAT 540 will quantify these) — an AI feature can win on RICE impact and lose on economics.

RICE shows the assumptionsthemes, not dateswon't-do = strategy
Problem set
  1. Take 8 candidate features (from your discovery + your own ideas). Score each with RICE, showing your work.
  2. Group winners into themes; lay out a 2-quarter roadmap (theme → outcome → candidate features).
  3. Write the "won't do" list with one-line reasons each.
Seminar
  • Defend one of your prioritization decisions against pushback — out loud, with the numbers.
  • When is it right to ship a low-RICE item anyway? What does that do to your credibility?

M5Metrics, funnels & instrumentation

A metric is a contract about what success means; choose it badly and everything downstream is theater. The north star is the one number that best captures delivered value — "weekly active learners," not "downloads" — because it measures the value users get, not the motion you make. Guardrails are the numbers that must not silently degrade: for AI features, always include model-quality metrics (eval score, hallucination rate, cost per session) — an AI feature that grows engagement while eroding trust is a slow-motion failure.

The funnel shows where value leaks: acquisition → activation → retention → referral. Instrumentation is the event log that makes the funnel visible — searched, asked, answered, rated, returned — and if you didn't log it, it didn't happen. Leading indicators predict the future (activation rate); lagging indicators confirm it (retention); a north star needs both. The AI-specific trap: AI changes quality, not just clicks — an A/B test showing +10% clicks while hallucination doubles is a loss dressed as a win. Your metrics tree (north star at top, drivers below, guardrails at the side) is the document that makes success legible to executives, engineers, and you.

M6Delivery: working with engineers

Discovery wins the problem; delivery wins the trust. The machinery: sprints (time-boxed iterations), standups (daily sync: what I did, what's blocked, what's next), retros (what to keep / change / try), and definition of done — the shared bar for "finished," which for AI features must include the eval gate, not just "code merged."

The PM's job during a sprint is not to manage people — it is to manage information: unblock ambiguity ("the spec is unclear here" → "I'll clarify by Thursday"), cut scope with evidence when reality bites, and protect the team from whiplash. Two disciplines matter most. First, tickets: slice work small enough to finish in days, each with acceptance criteria — a ticket without criteria is a hope. Second, saying no: engineers will propose technical debt and scope creep with good reasons; the PM's answer is "show me the impact" — the same RICE discipline from M4 at ticket scale. Engineers are expert collaborators, not order-takers: the best spec conversations change the spec, and the PM who treats engineering judgment as data gets better outcomes than the one who treats it as resistance.

Final · rubric · key terms

Course final

Take-home, ~2 hours, after Module 6. Open-book: this syllabus, your portfolio, the allowed readings. Graded within the Course project component.

Final examination
  1. Design an AI feature for a field-service company with 250 technicians who wait on hold for a support desk. In 700 words: the evidence you'd want before building, the feasibility judgment, the success metrics (north star + guardrails), and the top three risks. State your assumptions.
  2. Prioritization under pressure: an engineer proposes a two-week technical-debt refactor, a salesperson demands a custom feature for one logo customer, and a hallucination fix on tax codes has the best RICE score by far. Decide what ships this quarter and defend the decision — including the explicit "won't do."
  3. You are shipping a feature with no instrumentation. Define the measurement system you'd build before launch: north star, three guardrails with thresholds, and the event list — then explain how you'd know it's working at week four.
