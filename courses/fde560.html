Modules
M1The FDE operating model

Forward Deployed Engineering teams — the model Palantir originated and the industry has adopted — put engineers and operators inside the customer's world. They solve problems the standard product doesn't, they learn the domain by immersion, and they feed what they learn back into the product. The engagement lifecycle: land (find the real problem behind the request), scope (define what will and won't be built), build (deliver fast, in the customer's environment), deploy (roll out to real users with real workflows), scale (expand usage and value), harvest (turn the repeated pattern into product).

For an operator launching such a team, the PM-shaped roles are everywhere — the FDE lead is a product manager with a client attached: discovery, scoping, prioritization, stakeholder management, delivery, and risk, all in a customer room. AI multiplies both sides of the coin: the leverage — custom AI systems can now be built in weeks, not quarters, and the FDE team with AI literacy can deliver what used to take a product org — and the hype risk — every client wants AI, few have a problem that needs it, and the team that says yes to everything builds demos, not systems. This module builds the vocabulary and maps where judgment is required.

M2Scoping client AI engagements

The client says "we want an AI assistant." Your job is discovery for an external customer: find the actual problem behind the ask, quantify its cost today, and test whether AI is the right instrument — the AIPM 520 feasibility drill, run in a client room, with the client's numbers. The scoping instruments: the discovery workshop agenda (who's in the room, what questions surface the real problem, what "done" means to them), the feasibility memo — written before the SOW so expectations are set before the price is — and the statement of work: scope in/out, data access and security, eval criteria, success metrics, the human-in-the-loop design, and the operating handoff.

Three disciplines matter most. First, measurement in the contract: a scope that promises quality without defining how quality is measured is a lawsuit with a demo attached — the golden set and success bar belong in the SOW, not in a slide. Second, the out-of-scope section is the whole game: AI engagements live on scope creep — "can it also…" — and the SOW's explicit won't-list is your only defense that doesn't damage the relationship. Third, say no in the room: the client who wants AI without a problem gets told, with the evidence, early. The no is the deliverable that builds trust.

  3. Draft the SOW outline: scope in/out, data access, eval criteria, success metrics, HITL design, handoff — with the out-of-scope list explicit.

M3Delivery & evals in the field

Field delivery differs from product delivery in four ways. The client's data is messy and owned by them — quality issues you'd fix in a product org are negotiation items here. Their golden set doesn't exist yet — you build it in week one, from their cases, in their vocabulary, with their definition of "right." Their team must live with the system after you leave — which makes the handoff the deliverable. And the rollout is change management: the AI changes workflows, and adoption is a product problem with the client's employees as the users — training, feedback channels, and a champion inside the client org.

The field eval discipline: agree the success bar before the first demo (a demo can always be made to look good; a pre-agreed bar cannot be moved after the fact), design the human-in-the-loop for their operations — who reviews what, at what stakes, with what escalation — and produce the operating manual: how the system is measured, monitored, and maintained after handoff, with owners named on the client side. If the client can't operate it, you built a demo, not a system — and the reference call you didn't earn will tell you so.

their golden set, their baradoption = product problemthe operating manual is the deliverable
Problem set
  1. Design the field eval plan for your engagement: client golden set (20+ cases), scoring method, success bar, drift signals after handoff.
  2. Design the HITL: what the client team reviews, at what stakes, with what escalation path.
  3. Write the operating manual skeleton: monitoring, maintenance, owners, and the "what to do when it degrades" runbook.

M4From consultant to product

The endgame of FDE is productization: the third client who asks for the same thing is a market signal, not a project. The harvest discipline: what pattern repeats across engagements — the same architecture, the same eval templates, the same integration pain? What would a product version remove — the bespoke parts, the client-specific data plumbing, the custom UI? Where does the data flywheel live across clients — can anonymized patterns improve every deployment (with consent and governance, per DATA 530 and RSK 550)? The product is the pattern, minus the bespoke.

This is also the personal transition. The FDE lead who can spot the repeated pattern, scope the product version, and argue its economics is the AI PM — the same judgment, one level up: from client to market. Your capstone can be the first productized artifact of your own practice — a working prototype that embodies the pattern your engagements keep hitting. That is the difference between a consultant who delivers and a leader who builds: the consultant sells hours, the leader harvests patterns.
