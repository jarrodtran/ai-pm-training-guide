STRAT 540 — AI Strategy, Economics & Go-to-Market

The business layer: unit economics of AI, pricing and packaging under usage-based cost, positioning, moats, and the platform risks of model dependence. Where product judgment becomes business judgment.

Modules
M1Unit economics of AI

Software's founding assumption — marginal cost ≈ zero — breaks when every interaction burns tokens. The AI unit-economics model has three layers: cost per interaction (input tokens × input price + output tokens × output price, plus retrieval and storage), cost per customer (interactions per customer per month × cost per interaction), and the bill (customers × cost per customer — which scales with success). The strategic consequences: cost is now a design variable, not an afterthought. Prompt length, context strategy, caching, and model tiering are product decisions with price tags — the same feature can cost 10× depending on how it's built.

The discipline is the sensitivity analysis: "if usage doubles, the bill does X; if we switch to model Y, we save Z but accept W; if average conversation length grows, here's the curve." The executive who understands the model understands the strategy — and the PM who shows up with the sensitivity table owns the conversation. You built the working version in AIPM 520 M5; this module makes it a strategic instrument: what the numbers say about where the business can afford to grow.

M2Pricing & packaging

Pricing aligns revenue with cost and value — and AI breaks the old templates. Subscription pricing is simple and predictable but exposes you to heavy users: your cost is uncapped, your price isn't. Usage pricing matches cost but taxes adoption and surprises customers ("why did my bill triple?"). Hybrid — a base tier plus usage — is the common answer, but it pushes complexity into billing. Outcome pricing ("pay per resolution" — Fin's model) aligns vendor and customer perfectly and concentrates risk on your quality: if you can't resolve, you don't get paid, which is a beautiful forcing function and a scary balance sheet.

The Duolingo lesson from AIPM 520 stands: AI features succeeded and margins compressed; a premium AI tier at ~$30/month saw thin penetration (~5% of paid subs). The craft is matching price to the cost profile of the feature (M1) and the value it creates — and then testing the price like any product decision. Pricing is a feature: it has users (customers and sales), a UX (the bill), and bugs (bill shock, churn). This module's memo — a pricing recommendation with structure, rationale, and risks — is the document that gets you invited to the revenue conversation.

M3Positioning & competitive strategy

Positioning answers "why you, why now, why this product" in one line a customer repeats to someone else. The test of a positioning statement is not whether it's true — it's whether a customer would repeat it unprompted. The AI-specific distinction that organizes strategy: AI-native products are built around the model's capabilities — the model is the core of the product's value — while AI-wrapped products bolt AI onto a conventional product. Most AI "strategy" is wrapping, and wrapping is fragile: the feature is a prompt away from your competitor's version.

The durable moats in AI: the data flywheel (usage → feedback → better evals and products → more usage — from DATA 530), proprietary data and integrations, workflow lock-in, and distribution. Model capability is not a moat — everyone can rent the same model; what compounds is what you do around it. The deliverable this module: a positioning statement that passes the repeat-test, plus a moat argument that names which of the four moats you're actually building and how the flywheel feeds it. If the moat argument is "we use the best model," rewrite it.

M4Platforms, ecosystems & vendor risk

Build on someone else's model and you inherit their roadmap, their pricing, and their outages. Vendor risk is a strategy problem with three dimensions. Dependence: your product's quality is their model's quality — when they ship a worse version, your users feel it, and you can't ship a fix. Switching costs: your prompt libraries and evals are portable, but your retrieval stack, tooling, and integrations may not be — and every fine-tune is an anchor. Competitive exposure: your feature set is "a prompt away" from your vendor shipping it themselves — the platform that hosts your product can become your competitor with a press release.

The mitigations are structural: multi-model strategy (evaluate and keep the option — your eval suite from AIPM 520 is the instrument that prices every model), abstraction layers (isolate model calls behind an interface your team owns), and value capture outside the model — data, workflows, integrations, distribution, trust. The vendor-risk memo you write this module is the document you'll need at every company that builds on frontier models — and the questions it asks are the ones that reveal whether the business is a product or a feature of someone else's platform.
