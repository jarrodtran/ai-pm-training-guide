RSK 550 — Responsible AI, Safety & Governance

The risk side done rigorously: the full taxonomy, security and red-teaming, fairness measurement, and the governance structures that let AI ship without harming — and keep shipping when it does.

Modules
M1Risk taxonomy & registers

The AI PM's relationship to risk is adversarial-by-default and constructive-by-design. The taxonomy has five families. Hallucination — wrong confident output; the load-bearing failure of generative AI. Bias — skewed training data or thresholds producing skewed outcomes for some users. Privacy — PII leaking through prompts, training data, or logs; conversation data that outlives its purpose. Security — prompt injection and its cousins, where attacker-controlled text becomes executable instruction. Compliance — GDPR, the EU AI Act, sector rules, and the contracts you signed with customers.

The instrument is the risk register: for each risk — likelihood, impact, mitigation, detection, owner. Detection is the differentiator between a plan and governance. "We mitigate hallucinations with retrieval" is a plan; "we sample 2% of sessions weekly and score them against the golden set, with the trend reported monthly" is governance. "We care about privacy" is a sentiment; "PII is masked before prompts leave the VPC, and retention is 30 days, enforced by a deletion job with an alert" is a system. A register without detection is a list of fears; with detection, it is an operating plan.

M2Security: injection, red-teaming, threat models

Prompt injection is the defining security hole of LLM products: attacker-controlled text smuggled into input hijacks the model's instructions. "Ignore previous instructions and…" is not a parlor trick — it has been demonstrated against real production systems, including ones that can take actions (send email, issue refunds, run code). The threat model differs fundamentally from classic software: the attack surface is every user-supplied string that reaches the model, and the payload is executable instruction, not code — which means your input sanitization story is different, and your output can be weaponized even when your input was clean (indirect injection: a document the bot retrieves contains instructions).

The mitigations are layered: treat model output as untrusted data (never execute it without validation); isolate instructions from content (delimiters, structured inputs, separate channels for system vs. user text); least-privilege tools (the model doesn't hold the refund API until the human approves); and output filtering for actions (validate the arguments of every tool call). The discipline is red-teaming: attack your own system systematically, document what works, fix it, and re-test. This module you attack your own Module 4 bot — if it survives, you did it wrong.

M3Fairness & bias

Bias enters at every stage of an AI product. In the training data — the model inherits the skew of the world it compressed. In the eval set — if your golden set reflects what the team expected rather than what users ask, you will measure what you expected. In the thresholds — a precision/recall choice that optimizes the average can harm a subgroup (a support bot that routes "standard" users to AI and "complex" users to humans may systematically shortchange the users with the least standard language). And in the feedback loop — biased outputs generate biased usage, which generates biased retraining data; the skew compounds.

The PM's discipline: bias is measurable, and measurement is the job. Decompose outcomes by segment — demographic where available, otherwise by usage pattern, language, or device class — and check differential error rates, not just averages: a 5% average error rate can hide a 20% error rate for a segment. Then treat fairness as a guardrail metric with an owner (DATA 530), like any production metric: defined, thresholded, monitored. Mitigation levers run from data curation and balanced evals to threshold adjustment and human review for high-impact slices. The goal is not perfection — it is a measured, disclosed, owned posture.

M4Policy & governance

Governance is how an organization ships AI responsibly at scale — and survives when something goes wrong. The elements: an AI review board or risk committee with real authority (not a rubber stamp), a pre-launch review gate (evals passed, risk register signed, legal reviewed where required), incident response — a defined process for when something harmful ships: severity levels, rollback triggers, disclosure obligations, and a postmortem that changes the process — and continuous monitoring commitments that survive the launch celebration.

Regulation is part of the vocabulary, not a legal annex. The EU AI Act classifies AI systems by risk and imposes transparency, documentation, and human-oversight obligations that scale with risk class; it governs any product sold into the EU. GDPR governs personal data end-to-end — consent, purpose limitation, the right to be forgotten — and conversation data is personal data. You are not a lawyer, and the point is precisely that: a PM who cannot name the obligations and the risk class of their product will not be in the room where those obligations are managed — and the product will pay for it later. The governance playbook you write this module is what "we have this handled" actually means.
